Chart recipes

One feature per section. Each chart below is configured for exactly that feature and nothing else, and each snippet is complete — copy a block and it runs.

The Chart page is the opposite of this one: the surface in order, every demo sharing one dataset and one set of formatters. That shape is right for seeing what exists and wrong for learning any one thing. The written reference is docs/chart.md.

0. The rows

Twelve months, four fields. Every section from 1 to 12 draws these and nothing else, so the only thing that changes between them is the props. Rows are plain objects — no wrapper type, no Dataset, no adapter.

1. The smallest chart that works

Four attributes. No axis configuration, no margins, no colour, no tooltip wiring: the value axis rounds itself outward to readable numbers, starts at zero because bars encode by length, and the label margin is measured from the labels themselves.

2. Writing the values

valueFormat writes the axis and the tooltip, so a unit is declared once and cannot disagree with itself. labelFormat does the same for the x values.

3. More than one series

series replaces y. The legend appears on its own once there is more than one, and clicking an entry hides it — the last visible series refuses to hide, because an empty plot under a full legend reads as broken rather than as a choice.

4. Stacking

Series sharing a stack name stack together; a series without one never stacks. A name rather than a boolean, because a chart with two stacked pairs is a real thing and the boolean version ends up needing the name anyway.

5. Bars and a line together

Per-series type is the whole of combo-chart support — bars with a trend over them is two entries in one array, not a different component.

6. A second axis

axis: 'right' puts a series on its own scale. Each side takes its domain from its own series alone, and rightFormat writes its own unit. The grid stays on the left axis: two sets of gridlines at different intervals is a lattice a reader has to decode before they can read anything.

7. How the points are joined

Three curves in one chart, so they can be compared rather than described. smooth is monotone, not cardinal: the curve cannot overshoot its own points. The usual spline through 10, 90, 10 renders a peak above 90, which is a chart showing a value nobody measured.

8. The grid

grid takes 'y' (the default), 'x', 'both' or false. The default is not symmetry: a value gridline lets a reader carry a height across the plot, while a category gridline separates things that are already separate.

9. Turning the labels

labelRotate takes an angle, or 'auto' — which measures, and turns the labels only when the widest one cannot fit its slot. Rotation is a last resort rather than a default: turned text is measurably slower to read, so the axis thins first.

With rotation on, thinning stops: turning is what buys the room, so dropping labels afterwards would throw away exactly what the rotation was for. The bottom margin grows by the label's own length projected onto the vertical, or the longest one is cut off by the edge.

labelRotate: {{ rotateText() }}

'auto' measures against one slot, not against the axis — the question is whether a label fits between its two neighbours. So it depends on the width, which is why the demo below is in a deliberately narrow column: the same eight product names across the full page do not collide and are left flat. Widen the window and watch them straighten.

10. Where the axis starts

zero defaults to true for bars and false for lines, and that difference is not a quirk. Bars encode value by length, so cutting the axis makes a 3% difference look like 300% — the single most common way a chart misleads. A line encodes by position, where a zoomed axis is legitimate and often the only way to see anything at all.

yMin and yMax pin the axis outright, for the case where a chart has to be comparable with the one beside it rather than with itself.

11. A tooltip of your own

tooltip is 'shared' by default — every series at that x, which is what a reader is comparing. 'item' narrows it to the mark under the pointer. tooltipFormat returns a string: a tooltip that renders markup is an injection waiting for the first chart drawn from data someone else wrote.

12. Clicking a point

onPointClick hands back the row, the series, the value and its colour — enough to open a drawer or filter a grid without going back to the data to work out what was hit.

13. Animation

One clock per chart drives every mark, and the marks interpolate from where they were — watch the bars travel rather than redraw. animate turns it off, and prefers-reduced-motion turns it off without being asked rather than merely speeding it up.

animate: {{ animatedText() }}

14. A long series

240 readings, fifteen minutes apart. Nothing was configured to say the x values are time — a number past 1971 in milliseconds is read as time, and the ticks land on the hour rather than on the first reading.

15. Showing one part of it

brush puts an overview of the whole series under the plot with a window over it. The window is resizable from both edges, draggable in the middle, and a drag on empty track starts a new one — that combination is what makes it a control rather than a picture: narrow from the right, then nudge the left edge, without re-selecting from scratch.

It works for every cartesian type, because the window is what every mark reads. Nothing about the area below knows a brush exists.

16. Owning the window yourself

range and onRangeChange make the window controlled — inclusive row indices, so two charts can be locked to the same window, or a window can be restored from a URL. Omit both and the chart owns it, which is what the section above does.

Indices over rows rather than over time, deliberately. Markets close: on a continuous time axis a daily chart spends two sevenths of its width drawing weekends.

17. Pie and donut

Same data, same x, same y — one row per slice, its label and its value. Moving between a bar chart and a donut is one word, which is the whole reason the radial pair lives in this component rather than beside it.

maxSlices keeps the largest and folds the tail into one. The commonest way a pie fails is twenty categories, fifteen of them unreadable slivers; grouping the tail is what a person would do by hand, and unlike dropping them it keeps the total honest.

A donut's hole is not decoration: it removes the wedge apex, which is the part of a pie a reader is worst at comparing, and it buys somewhere to put the total. Setting the angles turns the same component into a gauge — a gauge is a pie you stopped early.

18. Candlesticks

ohlc names the four prices and volume adds the subplot under the price pane. The x axis is ordinal, not time, and that is the difference between a financial chart and a line chart with fancier marks: indexed by bar, "the last 60 sessions" means 60 candles rather than 60 days containing 43 of them.

Wheel to zoom, drag to pan. Zooming is about the pointer, not the centre — you point at the spike you care about, and it stays under the cursor as the window narrows.

Colour is decided against the previous close, not against the bar's own open. A session that opened below yesterday's close and recovered to just above its own open is a down day, and painting it green tells the reader the opposite of what happened.

19. OHLC bars

The same data, one word different. Each bar is a single path — the high–low stroke with a tick left for the open and right for the close — so 500 sessions cost 500 nodes instead of 1,500.

20. Sparklines

sparkline strips a chart to its marks — no axes, no grid, no legend, no tooltip, no margins. Axes on something 40px tall are unreadable furniture crowding out the only thing being said, which is the shape.

21. Metric tiles

<Metric> is the tile every dashboard rebuilds by hand, and rebuilds slightly differently each time. It is not a chart, which is why it is not a <Chart> type — but it composes one, so its sparkline is the same engine as everything above.

The judgement worth having is invert. A delta's colour follows its meaning, not its sign: churn up 5% is bad, latency down 8% is good. A tile that paints every rise green tells the reader the opposite of the truth on about half a real dashboard. The arrow carries the direction too, so the tile still reads in greyscale.
