Chart

One component, every cartesian chart. Responsive, animated, themed and keyboard-reachable without being asked — those are not features to opt into, they are what "a chart" means.

This is stage one: the engine (scales, ticks, the animation clock, the palette, tooltip, legend, accessibility) and the three marks that ride on it. Pie and donut, candlesticks and OHLC, and the infographic set come next, on this same engine.

Watch the bars travel rather than redraw — the clock interpolates from where each one was.

One line

The whole point of the API. No axis configuration, no margins, no colour, no tooltip wiring — the value axis rounds itself outward to readable numbers, starts at zero because bars encode by length, and the labels are measured so the margin fits them exactly.

Several series

series replaces y. Each entry may override the chart's type, its curve, its colour and its width. The legend appears on its own once there is more than one series, and clicking an entry hides it — the last visible series refuses to hide, because an empty plot under a full legend reads as broken rather than as a choice.

Stacked

Series sharing a stack name stack together; a series without one never stacks. A name rather than a boolean, because a chart with two stacked pairs is a real thing and the boolean version ends up needing the name anyway.

Combo

Per-series type is the whole of combo-chart support — bars with a trend line over them is two entries in the same array, not a different component.

A time axis, and a hole in the data

The x values here are timestamps, and nothing was configured to say so — a number past 1971 in milliseconds is read as time, and the ticks land on the hour rather than on the first reading. Load has four missing hours, and the line breaks there. Interpolating across a gap would draw a smooth climb through data that does not exist.

Smooth is monotone, not cardinal: the curve cannot overshoot its own points. The usual spline through 10, 90, 10 renders a peak above 90, which is a chart showing a value nobody measured.

Pie and donut

Same data, same x, same y — one row per slice, its label and its value. Moving between a bar chart and a donut is one word, which is the whole reason the radial pair lives in this component rather than beside it.

Nine categories here, five of them slivers — the shape that makes a pie fail. maxSlices keeps the largest and folds the tail into one, which is what a person would do by hand and keeps the total honest, unlike dropping them. Percentages are written only on slices with room for them; the rest are in the legend, which toggles like any other.

A donut's hole is not decoration: it removes the wedge apex, which is the part of a pie a reader is worst at comparing, and it buys somewhere to put the total. Setting the angles turns the same component into a semicircle — a gauge is a pie you stopped early.

Candlesticks

The x axis here is ordinal, not time, and that is the difference between a financial chart and a line chart with fancier marks. Markets close: on a continuous time axis a daily chart spends two sevenths of its width drawing weekends, and every candle carries a gap it did not earn. Indexed by bar, "the last 60 sessions" means 60 candles rather than 60 days containing 43 of them.

Wheel to zoom, drag to pan. Zooming is about the pointer, not the centre — you point at the spike you care about, and it stays under the cursor as the window narrows. The volume pane shares the same axis and the same colours.

Colour is decided against the previous close, not against the bar's own open. A session that opened below yesterday's close and recovered to just above its own open is a down day, and painting it green tells the reader the opposite of what happened.

OHLC bars

The same data, one word different. Each bar is a single path — the high–low stroke with a tick left for the open and right for the close — so 500 sessions cost 500 nodes instead of 1,500.

Sparklines and tiles

sparkline strips a chart to its marks — no axes, no grid, no legend, no tooltip, no margins. Axes on something 40px tall are unreadable furniture crowding out the only thing being said, which is the shape.

<Metric> is the tile every dashboard rebuilds by hand, and rebuilds slightly differently each time. It is not a chart, which is why it is not a <Chart> type — but it composes one, so its sparkline is the same engine as everything above.

The judgement worth having is invert. A delta's colour follows its meaning, not its sign: churn up 5% is bad, latency down 8% is good. A tile that paints every rise green tells the reader the opposite of the truth on about half a real dashboard. The arrow carries the direction too, so the tile still reads in greyscale.

What it does without being asked

Responsive — the width follows the container through a ResizeObserver; only the height is yours. Themed — every colour is a CSS custom property with a light default, so the palette follows the page's dark mode and any one slot can be overridden from an ancestor. Animated — one clock per chart drives every mark, and prefers-reduced-motion turns it off rather than merely speeding it up.

Accessible — the plot is role="img" with a generated label, and under 100 rows it is followed by a real data table, present for a screen reader and absent from the page. A paragraph describing a chart is not access; the numbers are.
